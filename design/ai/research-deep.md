## Part 1 – *Lethal Company* Creature AI 

Lethal Company’s monsters each use finite state machines, with states like **Idle/Patrol**, **Chase**, **Attack**, and **Return/Forget**, triggered by player actions (sight, sound, proximity). Their senses (vision angle/range, hearing range) and tells create life-like behavior. For example:
- **Bracken (Flower Man)**: *Stalker* state when the player is not looking, *Attack* when in range; *Idle* or *Frozen* when under player gaze. Eye contact forces it to hesitate. It apparently uses a “look-away” trigger: it only lunges when the player’s back is turned. (Speed and damage are unspecified; it likely moves at moderate pace when stalking.)  
- **Hoarding Bug**: Passive *Idle* until its scrap is stolen, then enters *Chase/Attack*. It simply stands by its loot and only reacts if the player grabs it. (Seems low HP and speed.)  
- **Snare Flea**: In *Idle* until activated by proximity, then *Drop* state: it leaps down onto players, initiating a *Suffocate* attack until shovelled off or the player escapes. It spawns on ceilings and despawns if the player exits the building.  
- **Bunker Spider**: A primarily *Patrol* or *Web-Lay* state (crawling around indoor ceilings). If the player enters webs, the spider transitions to *Chase*, attacking until the player frees themselves or kills it. (No exact speeds given.)  
- **Hygrodere**: Moves slowly in *Roam* (pooled slime on floor) with *Contact Attack*: any touch kills the player. If attacked, it splits into two smaller *Hygrodere* (i.e. a *Split* state). No ranged sensing – it just sits in place.  
- **Spore Lizard**: Normally in *Wander* or *Idle*. If a player comes close, it may enter a *Spray* or *Bite* attack: it releases vision-impairing mist and tries to nibble if provoked. Its tell is a visual puff of pink mist. It has low HP and speed.  
- **Coil-Head**: Alternates between *Idle* (frozen) when observed and *Chase/Attack* when unobserved. Effectively a “Pop-Out” state: it only moves and lunges when the player isn’t looking. (No random wandering – it stands still until triggered.)  
- **Jester**: In *Idle* until triggered (when player stays inside a room). Then it enters *Animate* (cranking music box) followed by *Pop* (instant kill attack). The music is the tell. (No avoidance – it simply begins its death sequence.)  
- **Ghost Girl**: Has a single behavior: it *Haunts* the last visible player, phasing through terrain from corridors to stalk them. It is invisible to others. (No standard sense model – it uses a special “haunt target” logic and will vanish or teleport if its target dies.)  
- **Nutcracker**: Normal *Patrol* when alone. When it spots players, it enters *Scan* (looking around) and then *Attack* with shotgun if the player is in sight. It taunts players by aiming first. It loses interest if its eye (the weak point) is shot.  
- **Thumper**: *Chase* state is a rapid charge in a straight line down corridors. It has limited agility: it ignores hearing, so hitting a wall often. If stunned (e.g. by sound), it pauses then resumes. It does not roam randomly – it always charges until blocked.  
- **Butler**: Slowly *Patrols* indoors humming (idle). If looked at too long or followed, it switches to *Stare-Back* (revenge) state and attacks. It never rushes aggressively: it moves only after being provoked. (It tolerates groups – it won’t attack if multiple players present.)  

Outdoors, the creatures include:  
- **Baboon Hawk**: In *Roam/Hunt* state, it patrols nesting grounds. On spotting players or being attacked, it enters *Attack* (closing distance to stab) and may call nearby hawks (*Summon* state) to mob. Their “taunt” scream is a pre-attack cue.  
- **Circuit Bees**: Normally *Idle* in a hive. If the player steals hive parts, they switch to *Swarm* state, homing in on the thief and zapping with electricity (they form an AoE field). Their tell is the buzzing intensifying.  
- **Eyeless Dog**: In *Search* mode, it wanders silently. Its hearing is highly acute: loud sounds (running, radio) instantly trigger *Sprint-Chase* and bite attack. Since it has no sight, it stays in *Search* if the player is silent. It retreats if stunned or the player hides quietly.  
- **Forest Keeper**: In *Dormant* state at night until triggered by noise. Once a sound is heard, it switches to *Rampage* and stalks the general area of the noise, crushing anything it catches. Its movement is fast and leaves no chance for evasion. (No lose-target state – it kills on sight.)  
- **Old Bird**: Static statue form until a player approaches. Then it enters *Screech* (awake) and possibly *Swarm* state: many birds emerge to mob the player. It is effectively an instakill – the birds flood area upon trigger. The tell is simply the approach of the statue.  
- **Tulip Snake**: Passive in *Idle*, slithering slowly. If cornered, it will strike quickly (low damage). Lethal Company treats it as almost non-threat – it flees or is easily killed.  
- **Circuit Bee**: (Outdoor counterpart to Circuit Bees above.)  
- **Maneater**: (Indoors) Transitions through three states as it “evolves” if taken outside: *Larva*, *Pupa*, *Adult*. Each has faster *Chase* and more lethal *Attack*. (No typical NPC senses; it simply transforms on leaving map.)  
- **Kidnapper Fox, Barber**: (Not documented in sources, but they presumably have special states: e.g. Fox might stalk and carry a player away.)  

**Why these feel alive vs robotic:** The monsters use multiple states with clear triggers and *telegraphed cues*. For example, Coil-Head and Bracken freeze when observed, so the player must unpredictably break line-of-sight to trigger them. The Jester’s wind-up music loudly warns of impending doom. Eyeless Dogs respond only to sound, creating imperfect information (if you stay silent, they may ignore you). Baboon Hawks exhibit pack tactics: one taunting call summons its pack for a coordinated attack. Forest Keepers and Earth Leviathans use the environment (sound of footsteps, ground tremor) to decide when to burst into action. In short, varied senses (sight vs sound vs proximity), delayed reactions, and special “tells” (audio/visual warnings) give monsters **personality and suspense**. The AI does not simply home in relentlessly; it has hesitation and conditional states, making encounters dynamic and unpredictable rather than mechanical.

## Part 2 – Proven RTS/Horde Unit AI Mechanics 

Modern RTS and horde games use a variety of well-known micro-AI rules:

- **Targeting & Focus Fire (StarCraft II):** Units have built-in *Attack Target Priority* values (higher ATP = more likely to be auto-targeted). For example, biological units might preferentially attack other biologicals or structures. StarCraft II also implements *smart fire*: units avoid shooting targets already dying (to reduce overkill) and only retarget when the current target is dead or out of range. A *chase leash* limits how far a unit will chase enemies (preventing them from straying too far from formation). Overkill prevention is partly manual (players focus-fire high-value targets) but some built-in logic exists (e.g. air units ignore dead ground targets).  
- **Auto-Acquire & Smart Attack:** In many games (Warcraft, SC2), idle units auto-acquire the nearest valid enemy within a certain radius. For example, a common rule is *auto-acquire radius ≈ 5–10× unit size*. Outside this, units default to moving or holding position. Similar rules apply in *Mechabellum* and *TABS*: combat units tend to pick nearest or easiest targets. Some games enforce **target switching** only when enemies move out of range or die, preventing erratic flitting.  
- **Chase/Leash Limits:** Units often have a maximum pursuit distance (e.g. 20–30 studs) after which they break chase to avoid endless pursuit. If the leader/unit of a group stops chasing, followers will eventually reset. This avoids pathing disasters.  
- **Movement Formations & Flow Fields:** High-level RTS (Supreme Commander, *Planetary Annihilation*) use **flow-field pathfinding**. A flow field is a grid of vectors pointing towards a goal. Each unit simply looks at the vector at its location and follows it, creating smooth group movement. *They Are Billions* (zombie horde) uses a similar idea: a global Dijkstra map (distance field) is computed from player base, and all zombies greedily move towards the lowest-cost neighbor. This allows thousands of creatures to swarm efficiently without individual pathfinding. Influence maps can also guide units by encoding threat or resource densities.  
- **Reynolds Boids (Swarm Behavior):** Flocking rules combine **separation, alignment, cohesion** forces. Typical weight ratios can vary – some implementations use roughly *alignment:cohesion:separation = 1:1:1*, while others heavily favor one rule (e.g. *1:1:100* to strongly repel units). These produce dynamic group motion with realistic spacing. In practice, AI units might calculate a weighted sum of (i) separation (avoid crowding), (ii) alignment (match neighbors’ velocity), (iii) cohesion (move toward group center) vectors each update. Fine-tuning (e.g. making separation much stronger) yields tighter but stable formations.  
- **Formations:** When units are given a formation move, they aim to maintain relative positions. Algorithms often use *flow fields* or *potential fields* with repulsive forces at corners to prevent units stacking.  
- **Kiting / Hit-and-Run:** Ranged units often fire then retreat if the enemy closes. A common rule: if health < **retreat threshold** (e.g. 20–50%), unit goes to *Flee/Regroup* state. During kiting, units will strafe (move perpendicular while shooting) – achieved by adding a bias perpendicular to the target when calculating movement.  
- **Focus Fire vs Spread:** Some games force units to focus-fire one enemy at a time (maximizing kill rate), while others randomize between targets. For example, *focus fire* heavily reduces enemy numbers quickly; *spread fire* distributes damage to avoid large damage wasted (overkill). RTS designers may explicitly alternate focus/spread modes depending on situation.  
- **Threat & Influence Maps:** Many AI use grid-based “heat” maps. A **threat map** stores danger levels (e.g. presence of enemy towers). Units can sample local threat to decide where to move (staying out of high-threat zones). Influence maps can also encode strategic value (areas of control or interest).  
- **Utility AI / Decision Scoring:** Systems like Dave Mark’s *Infinite Axis* give each potential action a utility score (based on curves) and choose the highest. In practice, one might evaluate: *“should I attack, retreat, or use ability?”* by scoring factors (unit HP, enemy density, distance). These non-deterministic scores help avoid predictability.  
- **AI Architectures:** Behavior Trees (BT) and Hierarchical FSMs (HFSM) are popular for game AI. BTs allow dynamic reactivity and easier authoring of complex sequences; HFSMs are simpler state machines. GOAP (Goal-Oriented Action Planning) uses planners to sequence actions to meet goals. Utility AI is another paradigm. For an RTS-lite game, a BT or HFSM is most common: e.g. a tree that first checks “am I under heavy fire?” to possibly retreat, else “is there a high-value target in range?” to attack, etc. Complex planners (GOAP) are usually overkill for simple unit tactics.  
- **Avoiding Robotic Behavior:** Many issues cause AI to feel artificial: *zero reaction time* (immediate state changes), *deterministic choices*, and *perfect knowledge*. Remedies include:
  - **Reaction delays:** Introduce a small randomized cooldown (100–300 ms) before changing actions, so units don’t twitch instantly.  
  - **Jitter and Randomness:** Add tiny random offsets to movement direction or targeting to avoid perfect alignment.  
  - **Hysteresis (Commitment):** Don’t switch targets/modes unless a condition is convincingly met. For example, once a unit begins attacking, require a threshold (like current target’s HP=0 or new target 30% closer) before it switches, to prevent flip-flopping.  
  - **Staggered Decision Intervals:** Instead of all units updating on the same frame, update each unit on slightly offset ticks (e.g. unit N updates on frame mod k) so they act asynchronously.  
  - **Imperfect Information:** Give units limited sight or noisy detection. For instance, attack only if target in front *and* not behind an obstacle, or allow a miss chance if target is moving.  
  - **Tells & Telegraphed Moves:** Provide wind-up animations or sounds before big actions (like Lethal Company’s Jester music or Thumper’s roar) so the AI “feels” like it is planning and executing. This also gives players a reaction window, making the AI feel fair.  

*Cited references:* The above draws on known strategies in RTS/micro guides and AI literature. For example, using Dijkstra/flow fields for hordes is standard (as in They Are Billions), and implementing jitter/latency is a known fix for predictability in NPC AI. The Lethal Company monster descriptions in  illustrate how audio-visual cues (tells) and event-based triggers make AI feel more “alive.” 

## Part 3 – Roblox AI Performance and Modules 

Implementing 64 AI units on a phone requires careful performance management: 

- **Heartbeat Budget:** Roblox servers run at 60 Hz (server Heartbeat). Each tick has only a few milliseconds of script budget (often <5 ms) before frame starts lagging. Per-unit AI logic must be very light. Batch-processing many units in one loop and minimizing heavy operations is crucial.  
- **PathfindingService Cost:** Roblox’s built-in PathfindingService is relatively expensive and subject to limits (e.g. a few dozen path requests per second per server). Each path request can take tens of milliseconds or more, especially on mobile. To optimize, *cache paths*, reuse them via *agent retry* (as in [SimplePath](https://github.com/TrendonRobinson/SimplePath) which repeatedly re-paths in small increments). Also, avoid real-time pathfinding for every unit; cluster multiple units to follow the same path.  
- **Spatial Hashing / Grid Partitioning:** To handle many AI, use a spatial data structure (e.g. grid or quadtree) to quickly query nearby allies/enemies. Roblox doesn’t provide one, so implement a simple 2D grid or cell-based hashing. Update grid occupancy infrequently (e.g. every 0.2s) to reduce cost. This accelerates neighbor searches for boids or collision avoidance.  
- **Level-of-Detail (LoD):** Lower update frequency for distant or idle units. For example, units far from the players or camera can skip complex logic, or use simpler behaviors. If 64 units, update only a subset each frame (e.g. 10 per frame in round-robin, giving ~6 Hz per unit). Use coroutines or queue systems.  
- **Network Ownership & Movement Replication:** To reduce network bandwidth, use `WorldRoot:BulkMoveTo` (or `VectorForce` with Anchored parts) and rely on Roblox’s built-in interpolation. BulkMoveTo moves parts on the server and replicates efficiently without firing *PropertyChanged* events for each step. Compared to setting CFrame manually each frame (which is expensive to replicate), BulkMoveTo is much lighter. Alternatively, use `UnreliableRemoteEvent` to send small movement commands (like target positions) to the client, allowing the client to smoothly interpolate. Using the new `NetworkOwner` API can also offload physics simulation to the owning client when appropriate.  
- **Open-Source AI Modules:** Rather than writing from scratch, existing community modules can be leveraged (respecting licenses):
  - **SimplePath** (MIT license) – a Lua pathfinding helper (uses PathfindingService in a repeated, incremental way). Useful for realistic chasing movement.  
  - **BehaviorTree3** (GPL-3.0) – a fast behavior-tree implementation for Lua. Can drive unit state decisions (attack, retreat, etc) with clear structure.  
  - **Boids/Steering modules** – Various boids implementations exist in Lua (e.g. on GitHub), often with MIT or GPL licenses. For instance, simple boid algorithms are posted on GitHub under open-source licenses. One example is [evallen/boids](https://github.com/evallen/boids) (MIT) which could be adapted. These provide separation/alignment/cohesion functions for flocking.  
  - **Utility AI modules** – Some community posts and GitHub repos (e.g. [UtilityAI](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/utility-ai/threads)) offer Utility AI frameworks (license varies; check individually).  
  - **Steering behavior scripts** – “Seek”, “Flee”, “Avoidance” steering behaviors are common (e.g. Pathfinding module docs show Seek examples). Also `NPC`-type libraries (some MIT/GPL on GitHub) implement basic obstacle avoidance and steering.  

Each of the above modules should be reviewed for license compatibility (SimplePath is MIT, BehaviorTree3 is GPL-3.0) and performance on mobile. In practice, a hybrid approach – server computes high-level decisions (state changes, target selection) and clients interpolate movement – yields best responsiveness with low network cost.

## Part 4 – Bot Opponent Strategy 

A fair AI opponent must make fusion/split and orders decisions based on visible enemy composition **without “cheating”** (no hidden bonuses). Key principles: 

- **Counter-Composition Logic:** The AI should monitor the enemy’s current forces. It uses the rock-paper-scissors: *6+ swarms clinging kill a Titan, Titans kill Brutes, Brutes kill Packs, Packs cleave Swarms*. Thus the AI fuses matter accordingly. E.g. if the player massed many Packs, the bot should create Brutes; if the player uses Titans, the bot splits into Swarms to cling. A simple algorithm is: count enemy units by class, pick the counter class, and build towards that (fuse until you can produce it, then split appropriately).  
- **Fuse/Split Timing:** The bot might have thresholds. For example, fuse to Titan if there are multiple enemy Brutes nearby; otherwise, stay as smaller units for mobility. If outnumbered by swarms, perhaps retreat or fuse to sweep. Experimental tuning (based on playtesting) would set exact thresholds.  
- **When to Order (Use Special Ability):** Each form’s “Order” (e.g. F4’s slam) should be used thoughtfully. For instance, only slam when a group of enemies is directly in front. The AI could check: *if 3+ enemies within 10 studs* then use Order. Otherwise wait. A simple cooldown (e.g. 4–6 s) prevents spamming.  
- **Reading Enemy Upgrades:** If this RTS-lite had any unit upgrades (not mentioned, but if so), the bot should value counters more as upgrades come in.  
- **Fair Difficulty Scaling:** Higher difficulties could get faster reaction or strategic bonuses *but not raw stat cheats*. For example, at Hard level the AI might slightly anticipate composition changes (e.g. start building Brutes when fewer Packs appear) or execute commands with lower delay. But it must obey same rules (same health, no auto-aim). Another approach is **handicap**: give the bot a 5–10% resource or build-rate boost (since games like StarCraft AI often get economy buffs at high level). Or give it one extra starting matter point. These are common in RTS AI to simulate smarter play while still visible to the player.  
- **Examples from Games:** *Clash Royale* AI (for practice matches) uses deterministic decks but balances them by timing and card draw randomness – it never has more elixir than the player. *Mechabellum* AI (deck-building RTS) reportedly tries to maintain a counter-composition (if the player builds melee, the AI builds ranged) and occasionally places units to soak fire. *Traditional RTS skirmish AIs* often use simple scripts: attack-move when over a threshold, defend at home if health low, build some counters. Many engines also allow scripting a build-order that adapts to reconnaissance (e.g. if you see X, build Y). 

In summary, the bot should **adapt its unit mix** to the player’s (e.g. by tracking last seen units), fuse/split to create the counter type, use Orders when strategically opportune, and possibly incorporate a mild difficulty advantage (faster decision or slight resource bonus) to scale. It must **not** see invisible information (no knowing hidden units) or auto-aim – it should operate only on what units are currently detected, possibly with a small reaction delay to mimic human response time.

## Top 10 Lessons: Making Unit AI Feel Alive for Young Players

1. **Distinct States & Transitions:** Give units at least *Idle*, *Chase*, *Attack*, and *Retreat* states (or similar), and clear triggers between them (e.g. “if I see an enemy, switch to chase” or “if hitpoint low, retreat”). Multiple states (like Lethal’s silent/pouncing modes) make behavior richer.  
2. **Telegraphed Actions:** Always play an animation, sound, or particle effect just before a big move. For instance, a short charge-up animation before a slam (like Jester’s music build-up) signals intent. Tells let players anticipate and react, making AI *feel* fairer and alive.  
3. **Imperfect Perception:** Simulate limited sensing. Use vision cones and hearing ranges so units can “miss” the player. This adds tension (players can sometimes slip by) and realism. E.g. blind (eyeless) enemies rely solely on sound, while others freeze if stared at.  
4. **Group Behavior:** Use simple boid/flow-field rules for groups. Let allied units form loose flocks (cohesion) or avoid crowding (separation). For example, Baboon Hawks call in reinforcements; mimic this with a small summon or rally ability. Shared Dijkstra maps or influence maps let hordes move smoothly as one.  
5. **Randomness & Delays:** Never have actions execute instantaneously or exactly the same every time. Add tiny random delays (100–500 ms) before state changes, or randomize movement direction slightly. For instance, a drunk-walking idle or slight aim inaccuracy prevents robotic uniformity. Even Lethal Company monsters jitter or pause unpredictably when idle.  
6. **Priority & Focus:** Implement priority targeting (like ATP) so units intelligently pick targets. Avoid “nearest always” blindly: prioritize threats (low-health, high-damage enemies) and focus-fire them. Also avoid shooting if target is already dead (overkill). These micro-decisions (common in SC2) make teams of units coordinate better, appearing smarter.  
7. **Contextual Ability Use:** Give each form a special move, but condition its use on context. E.g. only slam when enemies are clustered within range. This mimics “player-like” judgement. For kids, even simple rules like “use slam only when 3+ targets are ahead” suffice. It avoids spam and makes the AI look like it’s “thinking”.  
8. **Reactive, Not Predictable:** If an action just happened (like moving to chase), don’t immediately flip states on a momentary change. Use a short cooldown or health hysteresis before retreating or switching targets, so units commit to actions. This prevents annoying back-and-forth switching and feels more deliberate.  
9. **Sound & Visual Feedback:** Use sounds or particle effects to make AI actions felt. Lethal Company’s creatures have distinctive noises (growls, screeches). Even a simple grunt when attacking or a glow effect on alert makes the AI feel “present” in the world.  
10. **Simplicity & Surprise:** Complex AI isn’t always needed. Simple rules with occasional random branching can produce surprisingly rich encounters. For example, a unit might randomly “patrol” vs “pounce” states. When AI occasionally does something unexpected (like abruptly charging from cover), it feels alive. Leverage randomness in idle movement or decision (within reason) to keep kids on their toes.  

These lessons combine design principles (state machines, boids, utility decisions) with game feel (tells, delays) to create natural, engaging unit AI. By studying *Lethal Company* monsters and established RTS tactics, we ensure Alien Fusion’s units feel energetic and dynamic rather than mechanical.
